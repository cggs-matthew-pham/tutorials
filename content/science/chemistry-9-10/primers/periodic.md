## The idea in plain words

Electrons fill **shells** in order: 2 in the first, 8 in the second, 8 in the third (then 2 more for potassium and calcium). The electrons in the outermost shell, the **outer-shell electrons**, decide how an element reacts.

- Elements in the same **group** (column) have the same number of outer electrons, so they behave alike.
- **Group 1** (alkali metals) have one, easily lost: very reactive metals. **Group 17** (halogens) have seven, one short of full: very reactive non-metals. **Group 18** (noble gases) have full shells and hardly react.
- The **period** (row) number is the number of shells. A new period starts when a new shell starts filling.

## Where the picture breaks

- The 2, 8, 8 rule is for elements 1 to 20; heavier atoms fill shells in a more complicated order.
- The board's table shows only the first 20 elements and leaves out the transition metals between groups 2 and 13.

## Common misconceptions

- Noble gases don't react because they are gases (chlorine is a gas and very reactive).
- Elements in a group have the same total number of electrons.
- Group 1 metals are reactive because they are light.

## Periodic trends (node 3)

Each period fills one shell, so the period number is the number of shells. Across a period the outer electrons rise from 1 to 8, then reset to 1 as a new shell starts: that is why properties repeat and groups behave alike. Across a period, elements change from metals to non-metals. The table board can be coloured by shells, outer electrons, or metal and non-metal (`colour:`).

Misconceptions: outer electrons keep counting past 8; periods and groups swapped; the table is ordered by mass.
