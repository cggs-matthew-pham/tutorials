Classifying triangles

Name a triangle twice: by its sides (scalene, isosceles, equilateral) and by its biggest angle (acute, right, obtuse). Equal sides sit opposite equal angles, so some pairs are impossible: no right-angled equilateral triangle.

Roof trusses, bridges and set squares are all triangles, but not the same kind. Builders name them by their sides and their angles, because that tells you how they fit together.

Every triangle gets two names.

By its sides:

  • scalene: no sides equal;
  • isosceles: two sides equal (marked with the same tick);
  • equilateral: all three sides equal.

By its angles:

  • acute: every angle is less than 90°;
  • right-angled: one angle is 90° (marked ∟);
  • obtuse: one angle is more than 90°.

Equal sides sit opposite equal angles: an isosceles triangle has two equal angles, and an equilateral triangle has three, each 60°.

Explain it to me

Name a triangle by its sides: scalene (none equal), isosceles (two equal), equilateral (all three equal). And by its biggest angle: acute (all under 90°), right-angled (one is 90°), obtuse (one over 90°).

Equal sides sit opposite equal angles. So an equilateral triangle has three 60° angles, and can never be right-angled or obtuse.

For teachers

Students drag triangles on a dot grid and see them named by sides (scalene, isosceles, equilateral) and by angles (acute, right, obtuse), combine the two names, and reason about impossible pairs (AC9M7SP02, node 1). About 40 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can name a triangle by its sides and by its angles.
  • I can explain why some triangles are impossible, like a right-angled equilateral triangle.
  • I can use equal sides and equal angles to find missing angles.

Before class

Do the Run board yourself first. Students need the triangle angle sum (Angles in a triangle) and the words acute, right and obtuse for angles.

The one idea: every triangle has a sides name and an angles name, and equal sides sit opposite equal angles. The common mistake is naming by any acute angle (Zara's mistake): every triangle has at least two.

A square grid cannot make an exact equilateral triangle; the board counts sides within 2% as equal and says so.

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: take a show of hands; ask a 'yes' student to sketch one.
  2. Run: ask what the ticks and the small square mean.
  3. Investigate: for the impossible pair, ask what angles an equilateral triangle must have.
  4. Modify: for nearly equilateral, ask why the grid cannot do it exactly (the height is not a whole number of squares).
  5. Make: ask which angle is obtuse and why.

The hard part (on purpose)

Making a triangle with two names at once on the board. Ask students to say which name they will fix first and what has to stay true while they fix the second, rather than moving corners for them.

When students get stuck

  • A student calls a triangle acute because it has an acute angle (Zara's mistake). Ask: Which is the biggest angle? Fix: The biggest angle decides: under 90° acute, 90° right, over 90° obtuse.
  • A student gives only one name. Ask: What do the sides say, and what do the angles say? Fix: Name the sides first, then the biggest angle.
  • A student thinks a right-angled triangle can't be isosceles. Ask: What happens if you cut a square along its diagonal? Fix: Two equal sides at the right angle: 45°, 45°, 90°.

Stretch and support

Stretch: List all nine pairs (3 side names × 3 angle names). Which are possible? Sketch each possible one.

Support: Keep a two-column table on the board: sides names and angles names with a picture of each.

Where it leads

Next: The quadrilateral family (node 2).

Where it leads: Pythagoras' theorem (Year 8) for right-angled triangles, and congruent and similar triangles (Years 8 and 9).

What each step is for

  • right and isosceles?: Committing to whether a triangle can be right-angled and isosceles at once shows whether they see the two names as independent.
  • make one: Dragging corners until the board names the triangle 'isosceles, right-angled' shows the two names are separate choices that can combine.
  • two names: The sides name and the angles name are separate; Zara names the triangle by an acute angle it has, but acute means every angle is under 90°. Some pairs are impossible.
  • one change at a time: One change at a time on the board: from isosceles to scalene, then an obtuse scalene triangle, then nearly equilateral on half squares, then the edge case of two right angles.
  • naming triangles: Naming from sides, from angles and from both, mixed; one acute angle is the planted slip; the review keeps the angle sum fresh.
  • obtuse and isosceles: Building an obtuse isosceles triangle combines both names on purpose: the equal sides meet at the obtuse angle.
