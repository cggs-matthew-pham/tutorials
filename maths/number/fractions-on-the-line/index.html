Fractions and decimals on the line

Split each unit into equal parts and count from zero. A negative fraction or decimal counts to the left, so −1.5 sits halfway between −2 and −1.

A tide of −0.6 m, a share price down 1¼ dollars, a recipe with ¾ of a cup: numbers between the whole numbers, above and below zero.

Between any two whole numbers on a number line there are fractions and decimals: 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, 0.30.30.3, 1341\tfrac{3}{4}143​. Below zero there are negative ones too: −12-\tfrac{1}{2}−21​, −0.3-0.3−0.3, −134-1\tfrac{3}{4}−143​.

In this lesson you find out how to place them: positive and negative, as fractions, decimals and mixed numerals.

  • Predict: Read the problem and decide what you think before you try anything. A wrong guess is fine: it shows you what to look for.
  • Try: Try it on the board and compare the result with your prediction.
  • Investigate: Work out why it happens, one step at a time.
  • Modify: Change the problem on purpose and see what happens.
  • Practise: A few on your own, to check you have it.
  • Make: Make your own problem using what you have learned. Expect a few tries; that is normal.

Explain it to me

To place a fraction or decimal, split each unit into equal parts and count from zero. Quarters: split each unit into 4. Tenths: into 10.

A negative number counts to the left of zero. −34-\tfrac{3}{4}−43​ is three quarters left of 0. A negative mixed numeral or decimal is further left still: −1.5-1.5−1.5 is one unit left, then half a unit further left, halfway between −2-2−2 and −1-1−1.

Once they are on the line, read from left to right to put them in order.

For teachers

Students place positive and negative fractions, decimals and mixed numerals on number lines split into halves, quarters and tenths, including lines without zero in the middle, and order them (AC9M7N04, node 3). About 40 minutes.

Open the teacher guide: answers, solutions, slides and a worksheet.

Students will be able to

  • I can place positive and negative fractions, decimals and mixed numerals on a number line.
  • I can explain why −1.5 is between −2 and −1, not between −1 and 0.
  • I can put a mixed set of fractions and decimals in order.

Before class

Do the Run board and the Make task yourself first. Students need Comparing integers (further left is less) and converting between fractions and decimals, which this lesson assumes was taught in class (node 2 of the descriptor); the Investigate step opens with a quick check of it.

The one idea: split each unit into equal parts (the denominator) and count from zero, to the left for a negative. The common mistake is counting the part of a negative number back towards zero (−1.5 at −0.5); it is planted after the method, in Jo's working.

On the board, a point's readout gives the fraction and the decimal (−1 1/4 (−1.25)).

Running the lesson

  1. Predict: take a show of hands. Students who chose 'between −1 and 0' are reading −1.5 as −1 then +0.5. Do not correct it yet.
  2. Run: ask students to say where −1.5 will land before they let go of P.
  3. Investigate: if many students miss the quick check, do the quarters (0.25, 0.5, 0.75) together first. Then model the worked example live: draw a line from −2 to 1, mark the quarters, and count aloud from zero for −3/4 and −1 1/4. Reveal the prepared lines afterwards to compare.
  4. Modify: before the line from −2 to 5, ask: where is zero on this line?
  5. Practise: in the ordering question, students can write every number as a decimal first.
  6. Make: the board lands on −0.25; ask for other answers on paper (−0.21, −0.275). Then ask: is there a number between −0.3 and −0.25 too?

When students get stuck

  • A student puts −1.5 between −1 and 0 (Jo's mistake). Ask: Is −1.5 °C colder or warmer than −1 °C? Fix: Colder, so further left. Count 1 left, then half more left: −1.5 is further from zero than −1.
  • A student cannot place −3/4. Ask: How many equal parts is each unit split into? Which way is negative? Fix: Count the quarters aloud from zero: −1/4, −2/4, −3/4.
  • A student thinks −0.3 is greater than −0.25 because 3 is less than 25. Ask: Write both with two decimal places. Which is further left? Fix: −0.30 and −0.25: −0.30 is further from zero, so it is less.

Stretch and support

Stretch: Find a number between −1/3 and −1/4. Then explain why there is always another number between any two.

Support: Use the board for every question: the readout gives the fraction and the decimal. A strip of paper folded into quarters helps for the fractions.

What each step is for

  • where is −1.5?: Committing to a place for −1.5 before using the board tests whether they count the half towards zero or away from it.
  • place it on the line: Dragging P to −1.5 and reading where it lands settles the prediction: the half is counted further from zero, to the left.
  • count the parts: Split each unit into the denominator's number of parts and count from zero, to the left for a negative. Jo's mistake counts the 0.5 back towards zero.
  • one change at a time: One change at a time: a negative fraction, a negative mixed numeral, tenths, then a line that does not have zero in the middle, then the edge case of two names for one place.
  • place and order: Placing, ordering and comparing a mix of fractions and decimals; the review keeps integer ordering fresh.
  • always another number: Finding a number between two close numbers reverses the lesson: from the places back to a number, and shows there is always another one in between.
